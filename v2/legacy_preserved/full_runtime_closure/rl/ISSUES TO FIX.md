Issue 1: Trades Consistently Closing at a Loss (Few or No Winning Trades)
Diagnosis: The bot is repeatedly entering and exiting trades in quick succession, incurring transaction fees and small losses each time. The code shows that whenever the agent flips its position (e.g. going from short to long or vice versa), it immediately closes the previous position and pays a fee. For example, a BUY action will close any short position (if one exists) and then open a long, deducting fees from balance[1]. Similarly, a SELL will close an existing long position before opening a short[2]. These frequent reversals mean even break-even price moves result in a loss due to fees. Additionally, the reward function penalizes trades and transaction costs. In the environment, after each step the base reward is the portfolio value change minus a transaction cost penalty[3]. A small built-in trade penalty (TRADE_PENALTY) further discourages over-trading[4]. Because the agent’s policy has not learned to profit from trends, it tends to churn positions (likely due to random or poorly informed action selection), accumulating fees and negative rewards. Over many iterations, this produced almost no winning trades – the few times a trade moves in a favorable direction, the bot often exits too early (due to other issues below) or the gain is offset by prior losses. The net effect is a near-100% loss rate on trades.
Root Causes:
- Overactive Position Flipping: The PPO agent (or the MASA ensemble) is frequently switching signals. With no mechanism to filter out noise or hold positions, any minor change in predicted action closes the prior trade and opens an opposite trade, locking in a loss (fees + slippage) on each flip. The environment’s execution model doesn’t allow partial reductions or “hold” of an existing position without cost – any change in action incurs a close+reopen cost[1][2].
- Reward Shaping and Penalties: The reward function is likely always slightly negative for any trade that doesn’t move enough to cover the 0.1% fee. Since small winning moves are rare (and possibly cut off early by stops), the agent mostly sees negative rewards. The built-in trade penalty and drawdown penalty (risk-adjusted reward shaping)[5] mean that taking a position at all can incur a immediate small negative reward. A naive or untrained agent will appear to “learn” that every trade loses money – reinforcing the wrong behavior (either do nothing, or if it does trade, it doesn’t stick with winners).
- No Developed Strategy (Training Failure): Because the agent hasn’t learned a profitable strategy (see Issue 3), its actions are effectively random or reactive. Random trades in a market with fees will, on average, lose money. Without any winning bias learned, almost all trades end up as losers after costs.
Recommendations:
- Increase Action Stability: Introduce a mechanism to discourage rapid flip-flopping of positions. One approach is enforcing a minimum hold time for trades. Notably, the code includes a min_hold_minutes = 20 in the HedgePositionManager[6][7], intended to prevent closing a position before 20 minutes. However, this logic is not being applied to the RL agent’s actions in the current implementation (the agent can issue a CLOSE or reverse action anytime). Integrating this constraint – e.g. by using can_hedge_symbol() checks before executing an action – would block the agent from closing or reversing a trade that was just opened on a short timeframe. This gives trades time to breathe and potentially reach profit.
- Reduce Over-Trading Penalties Initially: While penalties for excessive trading and drawdowns are well-intentioned, they might be too punitive early on. If every trade is immediately penalized (e.g. a 0.2% penalty per trade plus fees), the agent learns that opening a position is worse than doing nothing. To encourage exploration of profitable trades, consider lowering or removing TRADE_PENALTY and high risk penalties during initial training. Let the agent discover that holding a winning trade yields positive reward before you re-introduce penalties for over-trading. Once it learns to find winners, you can gradually increase penalties to refine behavior.
- Allow Partial Position Adjustments: The current logic only has full open or full close actions for each symbol. Consider utilizing the increase/decrease actions of the 7-action space for smoother position management (e.g. scale out gradually instead of instant full close). However, note that the agent hasn’t learned to use those yet – training on partial exits could help it preserve some profit instead of turning winners directly into losers. Reward shaping (via the hedge reward calculator) already has a “partial profit” bonus for using DECREASE actions on profitable trades[8][9] – ensure this is active and weighted enough to encourage the behavior.
- Tune Transaction Costs in Simulation: If possible, simulate lower transaction costs or incorporate spread only, especially during training. The real fee is 0.1%, but if you effectively train the agent with zero or half fees initially, it may learn to hold trades longer (because small moves aren’t immediately net negative). Later, re-introduce the true cost and train it to overcome that hurdle with larger average gains per trade. In summary, make it easier to win in early training so the agent experiences positive rewards. Currently it’s in a scenario where almost every trade is a loss – which leads to either paralysis (no trades) or thrashing (since it can’t find a better policy).
By addressing the above, the agent should start to see some winning trades and learn patterns that yield positive rewards, rather than reflexively closing everything at a loss.

Issue 2: Trades Auto-Closing at the End of Each Timeframe Interval
Diagnosis: Positions opened on a specific timeframe (e.g. a 5-minute chart signal) are being closed as soon as that timeframe’s bar closes, regardless of whether the trade is still valid. In other words, if a 5m model triggers a long at 12:00, the position is force-closed at 12:05 even if price is still rising. This behavior is not intended in the code – the environment episodes are not supposed to end every 5 minutes (the default done condition is 1000 steps)[10]. This indicates a logic error in the multi-timeframe orchestration outside the core environment. Likely, the system is treating each timeframe’s signal in isolation and not maintaining the position across timeframe updates. The HedgePositionManager was meant to manage multi-timeframe trades (and allow concurrent holds), but it appears to not be integrated at the decision level. The configured minimum hold of 20 minutes in HedgePositionManager is being bypassed entirely (trades are closing after just one 5m bar, far below the 20 min minimum)[7].
Root Causes:
- Frame-Specific Agents Operating Independently: It seems the ensemble treats each timeframe as a separate “agent” that opens and closes its own positions. There’s no persistence of an open trade beyond the scope of that timeframe’s decision cycle. When the next bar comes, the agent either doesn’t realize a trade was open or issues a new action that inadvertently closes it. Essentially, the multi-timeframe consensus isn’t truly achieved – each timeframe model may be trading its segment of data in a vacuum.
- No Continuity in Signals: The intended design was that multiple timeframe signals must align to open a trade, and presumably remain aligned to keep it open. But in implementation, even if signals remain favorable, the position might be closed because, for example, the 5m agent “resets” each bar or a default action kicks in. We see a hint of this in the code: if something goes wrong in the hedge action decoding, it defaults to CLOSE_ALL as a “safe” action[11]. It’s possible that each new timeframe tick, the system by default issues a CLOSE_ALL (either due to a decoding issue or simply because the 5m timeframe agent finished its episode). This would explain the auto-closures.
- HedgePositionManager Not Enforced: The HedgePositionManager is supposed to allow multi-symbol, multi-position holding with rules (including the 20-min minimum hold) – but the RL trainer’s trade execution doesn’t appear to consult it when deciding to exit. There’s no evidence in the provided code that the RL agent’s chosen actions are vetted by can_hedge_symbol or min_hold_minutes. Thus an RL “close” (or policy flip) is executed immediately in the environment, ignoring the manager’s constraints. The environment itself doesn’t enforce hold duration – it will close immediately if told to (since it just executes the action given). The result is timeframe-based premature closure rather than the intended sustained hold.
Recommendations:
- Integrate Position Management Layer: Instead of letting the raw RL policy dictate every open/close directly each timeframe, introduce the HedgePositionManager as a gatekeeper. For example, when the policy (ensemble) signals a position change, check with HedgePositionManager: if an open position’s hold time is below min_hold_minutes, override the agent’s “close” signal and continue to hold. This could be done by modifying the action before execution (e.g., ignore a CLOSE_ALL action if min hold not reached, or treat a reversal signal as a no-op if too soon). By doing this, a 5m trade won’t terminate at the next bar unless there’s a very strong conflicting signal and the minimum hold time has passed.
- Persist Positions Across Timeframes: Ensure that an open trade remains active as long as higher timeframe conditions support it, even if the lowest timeframe oscillates. The multi-timeframe ensemble logic should be revised: Instead of each timeframe independently opening/closing, use a combined decision. One approach is a hierarchy where e.g. a 15m “core trend” signal opens a base position, and a faster 5m signal can only add to it or temporarily hedge, but not fully close it on its own. In code, this could mean treating a CLOSE_ALL from a minor timeframe as a suggestion to reduce exposure, rather than an immediate full exit. The agent’s 7-action space has DECREASE actions which could serve this purpose. In short, maintain state such that a position opened at 12:00 isn’t automatically assumed closed at 12:05; it stays until an opposing consensus builds or a stop is hit.
- Adjust Episode/Reset Logic: Double-check how the environment resets for each timeframe in training versus live trading. The log indicates the system uses 24 parallel environments (likely 4 per each of 6 timeframes). If each of those envs resets independently after X steps, it might inadvertently align with one timeframe’s bar. You may need to lengthen the episode length for lower timeframes or synchronize resets such that an open position in the 5m env isn’t wiped at bar end. Ideally, use one combined environment representing all timeframes (so that an episode reset only happens when all timeframe data has progressed sufficiently or after a fixed number of global steps). If that’s not feasible, ensure that a reset in a sub-environment does not equate to “close trade” in live trading. The live trading loop should maintain trades in an external state (e.g., via HedgePositionManager) independent of env resets.
- Logging & Debugging: It will help to add logging when trades are closed, indicating the reason (timeframe interval end, stop-loss hit, opposite signal, etc.). Right now, the absence of clear logs for position closure reason made it hard to pinpoint this. If you instrument the code to log “Closing XYZ position after N minutes due to [reason]”, you can quickly see if it’s always “timeframe interval ended” triggering it. That will confirm where the logic is and allow targeted fixes.
By implementing the above, trades should no longer be reflexively closed every bar. They’ll remain open across multiple timeframe intervals when conditions are still favorable, allowing the agent to capture larger moves (and actually realize winning trades). This will also give the RL algorithm experience with longer trade durations, which is important for learning trend-following behavior.

Issue 3: Lack of Learning or Adaptation Over Time (Static Behavior)
Diagnosis: After running for days and millions of timesteps, the agent’s behavior has not improved – it still behaves essentially as it did initially (closing trades quickly, no clear profit-seeking strategy). The training logs back this up: even after ~26 million timesteps and over 111k episodes, the “best reward” is still recorded as -inf[12], indicating the training loop never saw a positive result or meaningful improvement. In other words, the RL training is not effectively learning from experience. We need to determine if the model weights are updating at all, and if so, why the performance isn’t improving.
Root Causes:
- Training is Occurring, But Not Converging: The logs show that training loops are running (samples collected, PPO updates performed)[13], so weight updates are happening. The presence of many episodes and timesteps in the log indicates the PPO agent has been through plenty of iterations. The issue is that it’s likely stuck in a poor local optimum or not receiving useful learning signals. A combination of factors contribute to this:
- No Positive Reward Signals: As noted in Issue 1, almost every trade results in a loss or zero reward. The agent rarely, if ever, experiences a significantly positive reward for a good action. In reinforcement learning, this is catastrophic – with reward feedback mostly negative or zero, the gradient descent updates don’t get steered toward profitable behaviors. The agent may have effectively learned “do nothing” or random small actions, which yield near-zero reward, is the safest strategy. Indeed, if the best episode return is negative or zero, the trainer will never record an improvement (hence “Best reward: -inf” remaining).
- Reward Mis-Scaling or Mis-Definition: The training code might not be calculating episode reward properly, leading to the best reward tracking staying at -inf. It’s possible the code expects a certain reward range or completion signal that isn’t happening. For instance, if the environment never calls done=True until 1000 steps, and the cumulative reward is always negative, maybe the checkpoint logic only tracks positive improvements. Or it might be a bug where episode reward isn’t logged at all (hence best reward never updates from -inf). This could mask actual learning. However, given the observed behavior, it’s likely not just a logging issue – the bot truly isn’t improving its policy in a noticeable way.
- Complex State and Model Overfitting: The observation space is huge (multi-symbol, multi-timeframe technical features). The provided RTX5080FeatureExtractor constructs a large CNN for up to ~2000 features[14]. Such a high-dimensional input and complex model can be very hard to train, especially with the modest reward signals available. The PPO hyperparameters (learning rate, gamma, etc.) might not be tuned for this scenario. If gamma is near 0.99 and episodes are 1000 steps, the credit assignment across such long sequences with sparse rewards is extremely difficult. The model may simply be not learning anything useful; it might memorize trivial patterns or just output a constant action (which could appear as static behavior).
- MASA Ensemble Interference: The trainer uses a blended action from the PPO policy and a MASAAgent (an external model)[15][16]. If MASA is providing strong heuristic signals, it could dominate the actions, leaving the PPO to mostly follow MASA’s lead. This can stifle the PPO’s learning: essentially the RL policy might become a passenger, always second-fiddle to MASA’s decision. If MASA is fixed (non-learning) and not perfect, the ensemble might be consistently suboptimal, and PPO never learns to counteract or override it. In the logs, we see that when generating predictions, PPO outputs 52 signals and then MASA blending is applied[17]. If the reward from following MASA’s signals isn’t clearly better, PPO gets no gradient to prefer other actions. Worse, if the blending is done during data collection for training, the PPO is optimizing not for its own pure strategy but a moving target (the blend outcome), which can impede convergence.
Recommendations:
- Re-examine Reward Function and Scale: Ensure that the agent can eventually receive positive reward for a successful trade. You may need to adjust the reward function to be more forgiving. For example, consider scaling up profit rewards or giving a small positive reward for not trading when the market is unfavorable (instead of just penalizing trades). One idea is to use a shaped reward that includes incremental PnL and a bonus for hitting profit targets. The hedge_reward_functions.py outlines such bonuses (momentum alignment, partial profits, etc.), but those need to actually influence the agent’s training. Confirm if the HedgeRewardCalculator is being used during training rollouts – if not, the agent is only learning from raw PnL deltas, which as we saw are mostly negative. Enabling these shaped rewards could provide some positive reinforcement for desirable behaviors (e.g. opening in the right trend, as defined by momentum signals)[18][19].
- Improve Signal-to-Noise for PPO: With such a complex observation space, the trainer may benefit from hyperparameter tuning. Two things to try: (1) Shorter Horizons – reduce n_steps (rollout length) or the episode length so that reward feedback isn’t so delayed. If necessary, break the training into smaller chunks (e.g. train on 200-step episodes) so that events like trade closures provide a clear end-of-episode outcome (win or loss) more frequently. (2) Learning Rate and Clip – a very small learning rate might be causing extremely slow policy updates. Conversely, a large one might cause instability given noisy rewards. Experiment with an order of magnitude up or down. Also consider increasing PPO’s entropy coefficient initially to encourage exploration – the agent might be prematurely converging to a do-nothing policy. Monitor metrics like policy loss and entropy to ensure the agent is actually exploring alternatives.
- Phase out MASA during Training: If MASA’s guidance is overpowering the RL, the agent might not learn its own strategy. To fix this, you could decouple the training policy from MASA at first. For instance, train PPO alone on historical data (or in simulation mode) without any MASA blending, until it achieves some profitable baseline. Once PPO has a clue (e.g. it starts to get a positive average reward in backtests), then enable MASA blending to refine decisions. Alternatively, if MASA is meant to act as an “expert” to speed up learning, consider an imitation learning approach: have PPO learn to mimic MASA’s profitable decisions explicitly (using supervised or DAgger-style updates), rather than blending actions in a way that PPO doesn’t directly optimize. Right now, MASA is used at action time but PPO’s loss function doesn’t explicitly account for MASA’s suggestions – it only sees the outcome of the blended action. This indirection can confuse learning. Align the training objective with the blended policy or temporarily remove the blending.
- Better Performance Metrics & Early Stopping: Introduce a reliable metric for the agent’s performance and use it to adjust training. For example, track a rolling average of episode returns or win-rate. If after certain timesteps there’s no improvement, that’s a red flag to adjust parameters or training strategy. In your log, “Best reward: -inf” staying constant is a huge warning sign[12] that went unaddressed by the training loop. Implement logic to stop training early or change strategy if the agent isn’t learning (e.g. if after N million steps the reward hasn’t improved, consider halting and analyzing). This can save time and prevent reinforcing bad habits.
- Curriculum Learning: Start the agent off in a simpler scenario and then increase difficulty. For instance, begin training on 1-2 symbols instead of 10, or on higher timeframes first (which are less noisy) to learn basic trend-following. Once it performs there, add more symbols or lower timeframes. Right now the agent is thrown into a very challenging task (10 symbols * 5 timeframes with hedging) from scratch. It may be too much for PPO to handle without intermediate milestones. A curriculum could significantly help the agent learn progressively, rather than flailing in the high-dimensional combined space.
By making these changes, you should start to see the agent’s behavior evolve beyond the initial static pattern. In particular, watching the training logs after adjustments, you’d expect to see the “best reward” value become a real number (not -inf) and increase over time as the agent finds better strategies. That will confirm that training is finally making progress.

Issue 4: Dynamic Position Sizing Having No Apparent Effect
Diagnosis: The system’s dynamic position sizing module is designed to adjust trade size based on confidence, volatility, trend consensus, etc. In practice, however, all trades seem to use roughly the same position size, and the “dynamic” aspect isn’t yielding better outcomes (larger size on high-confidence signals, smaller on low-confidence). From the logs we can see that whenever a strong multi-timeframe signal occurs, the recommended size often hits a hard cap. For example, the log shows a case for AVAX where the model had ~97-98% confidence on multiple timeframes, which computed a base position ~13-14% of equity, but it was “Capped from 18.3% to 10.0% (max $250 per side)”[20]. The final size executed was 10% of equity – essentially the system’s global max. This pattern repeats (e.g. LINK also capped to 10%[21][22]). This tells us the dynamic sizing rules are active but always hitting their ceiling, meaning the agent never actually uses a size larger than the fixed max, no matter how strong the signal. Conversely, there’s no evidence it ever goes below a certain floor either (most trades might just default to that 10% band). In effect, dynamic sizing isn’t providing any tangible advantage – it’s behaving like a fixed position size system.
Root Causes:
- Overly Conservative Caps: The configuration for sizing includes explicit caps both in code and config. The HedgeActionDecoder limits base position to 8% in normal mode and 10% in “boosted” mode[23]. The dynamic sizing function then further clamps the target exposure via bucket rules: e.g. confidence <85% caps at 5%, >=85% caps at 8%, and only in the most extreme “trend lock” case (very high confidence across 3+ timeframes) does it allow up to 12%[24]. In practice, when the agent does get a high-confidence signal (like 95%+), it likely triggers the 8% or 12% cap. But then another risk layer (portfolio or decoder) knocks it down to 10% or 8%. The net result is that almost every significant trade is executed at the max of ~8-10% of equity. The dynamic calculation might suggest, say, 15% or 20% for an exceptionally strong signal, but it never materializes because of these safety ceilings[25][21].
- Lack of Low-Confidence Scaling: On the flip side, the agent’s trades in lower-confidence situations should be smaller (in theory maybe 2-5% of equity). It’s unclear if the agent is even executing trades at low confidence or if it’s mostly acting on high-confidence triggers. If it is trading lower confidence setups, it might still be using the default size. The code’s minimum position size is 2%[26], so it will never go below that by design. Thus the dynamic range is effectively 2% to 10% of equity. If most signals that prompt trades are above the confidence threshold, the agent will always be at or near 10%. So in effect, dynamic sizing collapses to a fixed ~10% size for most trades. This would explain why you saw no benefit – the agent never actually “bets bigger” on its best signals (it’s always at the cap already), nor does it significantly scale down beyond the floor.
- No Integration into Training:* Another subtle issue – the RL agent itself may not be aware of the dynamic position sizing logic at all. During training in the environment, the execute_trades function uses a fixed 10% of balance for any trade[27]. The environment doesn’t simulate the variable sizing; it just always takes 0.1 of balance. That means the agent was trained assuming a fixed size. The dynamic sizing module is only used at runtime in the ensemble logic (after the agent outputs a confidence). This disconnect can cause suboptimal outcomes. For instance, the agent might output a high “confidence” action arbitrarily (since in training confidence didn’t change size), and the live system then upsizes the trade – potentially taking on more risk than the agent ever trained for. Conversely, the agent might not bother to differentiate between a 70% and 95% confidence prediction, because in training both resulted in the same outcome (enter a position of fixed size). So the PPO policy’s notion of “confidence” may not be calibrated to what the dynamic sizing expects. In short, the agent wasn’t explicitly trained to optimize position sizing decisions, so the dynamic sizing layer is working with signals that weren’t tuned for it.
Recommendations:
- Relax the Caps (Gradually): The protective caps on position size are likely too tight for the system to ever utilize “dynamic” sizing. You might consider raising the caps – for example, allow up to 15% or 20% position when multiple timeframes line up, and perhaps up to 10% in normal cases. Since the code currently sets 8% base and 10% boosted[26], try bumping these to maybe 15% base and 25% boosted, and adjust bucket caps in compute_position_sizing accordingly (the lines that set 0.08/0.12 could be, say, 0.15 for high confidence, 0.10 for medium). Important: do this carefully in simulation/backtest first – larger positions mean larger risk. But without increasing these, every “strong” signal will continue to just max out at the same size, and you won’t see the intended effect of scaling.
- Enable True Dynamic Behavior in Training: Ideally, incorporate the position sizing into the training process so the agent learns to output appropriate confidence levels or sizing actions. One approach is to include the size as part of the action space (which you started with the HedgeActionDecoder continuous outputs). Ensure that during training, the network’s size output actually impacts the trade outcome in the simulation. For example, you could modify the environment to use the agent’s suggested size_suggest when executing trades (rather than the hard-coded 10%). If that’s complex to do directly, at least train multiple scenarios with different fixed fractions (5%, 10%, 15%) so the agent experiences how larger size yields bigger PnL swings. Right now, the agent doesn’t “feel” the difference between a small and big position, so it has no incentive to refine those outputs.
- Dynamic Sizing as a Function of Proven Edge: Another strategy is to tie position size to the agent’s recent performance or signal quality. For instance, use the continuous learner’s performance metrics: if the model has been doing well (Sharpe > threshold), then allow scaling up. Currently, the code hints at adjusting MASA weight by regime and confidence, and the position sizing uses performance (perf_score) as a minor factor[28]. You could amplify that: e.g., increase target_exposure when the agent has a high win rate lately, and decrease after a losing streak (this is a bit like a self-tuning Kelly criterion). This wasn’t explicitly implemented, but you have the building blocks (the _trade_performance tracking in HybridTrainer could feed into position sizing to dynamically modulate exposure based on demonstrated accuracy[29][30]). This way, if the agent is static because it’s unsure, it won’t bet big; but when it finally gains an edge, the system will naturally start increasing size.
- Verify Low-Confidence Behavior: Make sure that when signals are weak or mixed, the agent actually reduces position size (or doesn’t trade at all). It might be doing so already (since 1m timeframe is “learning only” and at least 3 timeframes are required for confirmation per your rules[31][32]). But if you find it’s entering trades even on flimsy signals (and still using ~10%), then there’s an issue in the gating logic. Double-check the gating: the code indicates needing 3+ action timeframes ≥80% confidence[33] for trend_lock, and presumably you intended not to trade if fewer agree. Ensure that condition is enforced – i.e., require a minimum consensus or confidence (perhaps MIN_TRADING_CONFIDENCE) before any trade. The config mentions MIN_TRADING_CONFIDENCE which likely should be set (maybe 0.6 or 0.7). If not properly enforced, the agent might be overtrading low-quality signals at the default size. Setting a higher confidence threshold for entry can naturally lead to fewer trades but with higher average confidence (hence allowing dynamic sizing to shine).
- Monitor Position Size Utilization: After implementing changes, watch the logs for how often the “capped at X%” message appears. The goal is to see trades that sometimes use less than the cap and occasionally more when warranted (if you raise the cap). If all trades still cluster at the new cap or the minimum, then the sizing function may need further tweaking. Ideally, you want a spectrum: e.g. some trades at 5%, some at 10%, some at 15% of equity – corresponding intuitively to the strength of setup. If that spectrum isn’t appearing, the logic might still be too restrictive or the agent’s confidence outputs might be saturating (e.g., always outputting very high confidence regardless). In the latter case, consider calibrating the confidence output of the network (maybe the policy’s softmax is almost always peaking near 1.0 for the chosen action, which is common early in training). Temperature scaling or entropy regularization can keep the policy from being overconfident in its outputs. Ensuring the confidence estimates are well-distributed will make the dynamic sizing more meaningful.
By making these adjustments, dynamic position sizing should start to have the intended effect: when the stars align (multiple timeframes in agreement, strong trend, low volatility), the bot will allocate a bigger position and thus reap bigger profits from those rare opportunities. In choppier or uncertain conditions, it will scale down or stay on the sidelines, cutting losses by not overexposing. This adaptivity is key to improving the overall profitability of the strategy.

Issue 5: Overly Aggressive Stop-Losses Causing Premature Exits
Diagnosis: The system is cutting losses very frequently, indicating that the stop-loss logic is too aggressive or misaligned with the strategy’s signals. You observed many trades stopped out one after another (“repeated stop-losses”), which suggests the stop parameters are too tight given normal market noise. The code confirms a very conservative risk management approach: for example, the HedgePositionManager imposes a daily loss limit of 5% – if triggered, it will start closing positions to reduce exposure[34]. Furthermore, the advanced risk management computes per-trade stop thresholds on the order of only a few percent. In the advanced_risk_management logic, depending on market regime, the stop-loss could be as low as ~2–5% in some cases (for volatile or uncertain conditions) and at most ~15–20% in trending scenarios[35][36]. A 2-5% stop on a short timeframe like 5m is extremely tight – it’s common for price to wiggle that much and then resume the trend. The trailing stop is also set to activate at just 2% profit with a 1% trail step[37], which, while intended to lock in profits, could be kicking in very soon after a trade finally goes green, often resulting in breakeven or small wins instead of letting winners run. Overall, the protective measures are likely too sensitive, causing the system to exit trades before they have a chance to succeed.
Root Causes:
- Mis-tuned Volatility Estimates: The risk logic uses volatility (e.g., ATR or similar) to set stops. If the volatility estimate is off or too low, the computed stop range will be too tight. For instance, if the system thinks current volatility is 1%, it might set a stop at 2% – but if actual volatility spikes or the asset has larger wicks, 2% is nothing. We saw in logs for AVAX that vol=0.500 (likely meaning 5m ATR is 0.5% of price)[38], which might have led the system to pick a ~2% stop (since 2 * 0.5% = 1% min, but with some floor maybe 2%). Indeed, AVAX and others hit those stops repeatedly because crypto can move a few percent in minutes then bounce back. Essentially, the stop-loss formula might not account for the “whiplash” nature of lower timeframes.
- No Differentiation by Timeframe or Context: The same stop logic might be being applied uniformly, regardless of timeframe. A 5% stop on a daily chart is fairly tight; on a 5m chart, it’s extremely tight. If the RiskLimits module doesn’t scale stops by timeframe properly, then short-term trades are unfairly constrained. Similarly, if the agent is counter-trend or mean-reversion at times, tight stops will always get hit because mean-reversion strategies often require wider stops (they profit from bounces after dips, which needs room). The one-size-fits-all stop approach is likely not in sync with the strategy the RL is trying (or failing) to execute.
- Lack of Coordination with Entry Logic: Ideally, stop losses should be placed at logical invalidation points (e.g., below a support level for a long trade). The RL agent’s signal might inherently expect some oscillation. But the risk management doesn’t “know” the strategy’s intent – it applies generic thresholds. This mismatch means the agent might take a trade expecting a +10% move with a tolerance for -5% drawdown, but the RiskLimits will cut it at -2% because volatility at entry was low. The agent then registers a loss even if its idea would’ve worked given more room. Over time, the agent might stop taking those trades (which could have been winners) because it learns they always hit the stop. In effect, the overly tight stops are sabotaging both live performance and the learning process.
- Trailing Stop Too Early: The trailing stop kicking in at 2% profit might also convert potential big winners into small ones. For example, a trade might go +3% in profit (trigger trailing stop to move to +2% lock-in) then pull back 2% to hit the trail, yielding a tiny +0.5% after fees – then price takes off +10% without the bot on board. This scenario might be happening frequently. While some profit was secured, the strategy never catches a large trend because the trail is essentially too tight to ever let profits run. Over time this yields a lot of small wins and many small losses – the classic churn with no big payoff. That could explain why no large winning trades appear to offset the stops.
Recommendations:
- Widen Stop-Loss Parameters: Revisit the volatility-based stop formulas. If currently the stop threshold is max(2%, min(5%, k*volatility)) for some k, consider increasing those numbers. For short-term trades, you might allow 5–8% minimum stop, and up to 15%+ in volatile conditions. Essentially, give the trade more breathing room. It’s better to risk a bit more per trade but actually let a good position play out, than to cut every trade at the first hint of loss. Empirically determine the typical pullback range for your strategies and set stops just beyond that. The regime-based logic you have is good (wider stops in trending markets, tighter in flat markets), but even the “wider” might be too small currently. Try, for example, making the “trend” regime stop ~20% and the normal regime ~10%. These might sound high, but remember, position sizing is small (8-10% equity), so a 10% move against the position is only ~1% hit to equity – acceptable if winners are larger.
- Separate Stop Profiles per Timeframe: A 5m trade likely needs a different stop strategy than a 4h trade. You can implement a rule where lower timeframe signals use a looser stop relative to their expected reward, or perhaps rely more on the trailing stop than a fixed stop. For instance, you could decide: for 5m/15m initiated trades, set an initial stop at, say, 0.5x the daily ATR (which could be ~3-5%), whereas for a 1d trade, 0.5x daily ATR might be 10% – and that’s fine. Tailor the RiskLimits: it could accept a timeframe parameter and adjust the loss_threshold calculation accordingly. Currently, it appears uniform. By tuning it, you avoid choking off the short-term trades prematurely.
- Integrate Strategy Context: If possible, incorporate some of the agent’s signal context into stop placement. Since you have a rich feature set (momentum, exhaustion signals, etc.), you could use those to place smarter stops. For example, if momentum is high and positive on entry, perhaps use a tighter stop (assuming if momentum flips, trade is invalid). If momentum is low or it’s a mean-reversion entry, use a looser stop (since noise is expected). Another idea: if multiple timeframes agree (trend-lock), maybe you trail the stop wider initially because you expect a big move. These nuanced approaches align the stop logic with the trade rationale, reducing the chance of false stop-outs.
- Adjust Trailing Stop Strategy: Consider delaying the activation of the trailing stop or using a larger activation threshold for bigger trends. Instead of activating at 2% profit, you might not want to trail until, say, 5% profit, with a 2% step – this gives more room for a trade to develop into a big winner. Another approach is a partial take-profit combined with a looser runner: your DynamicTakeProfit is set to take 33% off at +10%, +20%, etc.[39]. Perhaps you should rely more on those partial TPs for securing early profit, and then allow the remainder position to run with a very loose trail (or no trail until a much higher gain). This way, you bank something but still have skin in the game for the big move. Right now, the trailing stop of 2% is effectively acting like a very tight leash – easing it could substantially improve risk-reward outcomes.
- Monitor and Iterate: After softening the stop-loss rules, monitor the outcomes. You should see a decrease in the number of trades closed at a small loss and hopefully an increase in average win per trade. Watch metrics like maximum adverse excursion (how far trades go negative before turning positive) to ensure your new stops cover those typical dips. If you find, for example, most winning trades went as far as -4% against you before turning around, then a 5% stop makes sense; a 2% stop was cutting them all off. Also monitor if widening stops causes any single loss to grow unacceptably – if so, you might need to concurrently reduce position size a bit to compensate for larger stop distance (so that risk per trade remains tolerable). It’s a trade-off, but given the current situation (lots of tiny losses), the adjustment should tilt things in favor of giving trades a chance to become winners.
In summary, loosen the straitjacket on your trades. The current stop-loss and trailing configurations, while safe on paper, are so tight that they virtually guarantee many stopped-out trades and very few big winners. By recalibrating these to market reality (especially on low timeframes), the bot will stay in positions longer and only exit when the trade truly goes wrong, not just on a minor normal retracement. This should markedly improve the win rate and the profit factor of the system.
